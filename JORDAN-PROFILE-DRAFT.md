## Jordan's backstory (hidden from learners)

### What's in use today
From Prompt 1 (`prompts/employee.js`), in Jasmin's words:

> You recently became the main caregiver for a parent, and mornings have become unpredictable. You feel embarrassed and are worried it will hurt your standing.

Jordan reveals it only if the manager earns trust through genuine, open questions (openness level 4 hints, level 5 shares).

### A fuller version (suggested details, for Jasmin to edit)
Fixed details mean every learner meets the **same Jordan**, which matters when a class compares notes. Anything here can change.

**The situation**
- About two months ago, Jordan's mom moved in after a fall. Jordan is now the main caregiver; a sibling lives out of state and helps with money but not day to day.
- Jordan's mom needs help getting up, getting dressed, and taking morning medication.
- A home health aide is supposed to arrive at 8:00, but often comes late or cancels, so Jordan can't leave until someone is there.
- There are frequent doctor's appointments, usually scheduled in the morning.

**How it shows up at work**
- Late arrivals: 15–30 minutes, on mornings when the aide is late. (The scenario card says four times in two weeks.)
- Missed deadlines: Jordan has been working late at night to catch up, is exhausted, and let two internal deadlines slip.
- Hasn't told anyone at work. Has quietly been skipping lunch to make up time.

**How Jordan feels**
- Embarrassed: Jordan has always been the reliable one, and is proud of that.
- Worried that asking for help will look like an excuse, or hurt the chance of a promotion Jordan has been hoping for.
- Protective of mom's privacy. Will not want to share medical details.
- Relieved, and a little emotional, if the manager is genuinely kind.

**What would actually help** (all within the HR policies)
- A later start, for example 9:30 or 10:00, within core hours (HR-210, manager can approve).
- One or two remote mornings a week (HR-210, manager can approve).
- A temporary lighter workload while things settle, up to 4 weeks (HR-210); longer needs HR.
- Information about family and caregiver leave, or a more formal arrangement (HR-305, refer to HR).
- The Employee Assistance Program's eldercare resources (HR-410).

**What Jordan would do in the plan**
- Agree readily to a later start or remote mornings.
- Ask questions about anything formal ("Would HR need to know details?").
- Push back gently if the plan feels like punishment, or if a check-in feels like being watched.
- Commit to messaging before start time on bad mornings (HR-104).

**What makes Jordan shut down**
- Being told "you're always late", or anything that sounds like a label.
- Threats or consequences early in the conversation.
- Being asked about mom's medical details.
- Feeling rushed, like the manager is between meetings.

## 1. Personality variations ⭐ (Jasmin's favorite)

The same scenario and the same hidden backstory, but a different personality. An instructor could assign different versions in a class, then compare how the same approach played out with different people. **The lesson for managers: there's no one script; adapt to the person.**

Starter ideas:
- **Guarded:** short answers, slow to trust, needs real patience before opening up.
- **Chatty and deflecting:** friendly and talkative, uses humor and changes the subject when things get personal.
- **Quiet and apologetic:** says sorry a lot, agrees quickly, but may not say what's really going on unless asked gently.
- **Defensive and direct:** pushes back ("I've still been getting my work done"), but opens up if the manager stays calm and fair.

Questions to decide:
- Does the learner choose a personality, does the instructor assign it, or is it random?
- Does the learner know which version they got, before or after?
- Should the coach adjust (for example, credit patience more with the guarded version)?
- Does this connect to difficulty levels (for example, "guarded" = hard)?
- Group debrief prompt: "Who had which Jordan? What worked with yours, and what didn't?"

## 2. "Know your employee" profile (visible to learners, current version)

A third card in **Step 1, Prepare**. It must not hint at the hidden backstory.

**Current wording (in review):**

> **Know your employee: Jordan**
> - **Role:** Project Coordinator, 3 years on the team. Manages project timelines and prepares materials for client meetings.
> - **Track record:** Strong performance reviews every year. Reliable with deadlines, and teammates often go to Jordan for help.
> - **Communication style:** Quiet in group meetings, more open one-on-one. Appreciates honesty. Takes a little time to open up; if she feels judged, she gets brief and guarded.
> - **What Jordan values:** Being trusted with responsibility, and doing careful, reliable work.
> - **Your working relationship:** Good. You meet for monthly 1:1s, and the conversation sometimes drifts to personal topics, like weekend plans, but stays surface level.

Why: track record and values make the recent change stand out; communication style cues a private, respectful approach; the relationship line shows real rapport while explaining why the manager doesn't already know what's going on. Nothing about home, family, health, or mornings.

Open questions: Jordan's pronouns (name-only for now); whether the coach credits adapting to Jordan's style.
